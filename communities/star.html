Star, Idaho

A farm town on the north bank of the Boise River that roughly doubled in a decade, and is now rebuilding its road network to match.

Living in Star

The Highway Arrived After the Houses

Most Treasure Valley towns grew outward from a main street. Star did too, but the last ten years compressed what usually takes forty. The historic core along State Street is still only a few blocks deep. Almost everything else you'll drive past — the subdivisions north of the highway, the ones still framing on former hay ground — went in after 2015.

That sequence explains a lot about how Star feels today. The housing came first and the infrastructure is catching up. The Idaho Transportation Department is widening State Highway 16 between Highway 44 and Beacon Light Road to four lanes with a median barrier, and adding an overpass at Floating Feather Road. When that corridor is finished, the drive from Star toward the Chinden and I-84 job centers changes meaningfully. Until then, Highway 44 carries most of the load, and it shows at commute hours — something Star's own mayor has said publicly rather than tried to talk around.

The Land Itself

Star sits on flat, irrigated ground on the north side of the Boise River, roughly between Eagle and Middleton. There are no foothills inside the city. The terrain is agricultural bench land, which is precisely why it built out so quickly: the parcels were large, level, already served by irrigation, and comparatively simple to plat.

The river forms the southern edge of town and is the reason a settlement landed here in the first place, though it's worth being straight about this — for most people buying in Star, the river is somewhere you drive to on a Saturday, not something you see from the kitchen window. Actual river frontage is limited and priced accordingly.

What Gets Built Here

Star's inventory skews new. Two-story production homes on lots in the six- to ten-thousand-square-foot range make up much of what trades, generally from builders working several subdivisions at once. Move a mile or two out from the center and the pattern breaks up: half-acre and acre parcels, some older ranch-style houses on irrigated ground, the occasional small horse property that predates the subdivisions around it.

That outer ring is where Star still resembles what it was, and it's the part of the market that's hardest to replace once it's gone. Buyers looking for acreage in Ada County without driving to Kuna tend to end up looking here.

Practical Considerations

  • Highway 44 is the primary route east toward Eagle and Boise, and it backs up at peak hours. Off-peak, downtown Boise is a reasonable drive; at 5pm it is not the same trip.
  • Star is in Ada County, which puts it in a different county, school, and highway district than Middleton just to the west — worth understanding before comparing tax bills.
  • City services and road capacity have been running behind the building permits. Star has been open about the budget pressure that creates.
  • Highway 16 improvements are underway but not finished. Buying here partly means buying the corridor's future.

How It Compares Nearby

Eagle, immediately east, has the foothills, an established downtown, and a comprehensive plan currently working to limit how far development climbs. Star has the opposite posture — a city government trying to catch up with growth rather than slow it. Middleton, west across the county line, has held onto more working farmland and a more agricultural feel. Star sits between those two conditions and is moving steadily toward the Eagle end of the spectrum.
